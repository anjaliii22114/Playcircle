Why playing together matters

Play is not only for fun. When children and adults play in groups, they practise taking turns, communicating, solving problems and handling wins and losses. Shared play also creates memories and helps people feel they belong.

Social skills

Sharing, listening, cooperating and resolving small disagreements.

Physical activity

Running, jumping, throwing and balancing, usually without feeling like exercise.

Thinking skills

Planning, remembering rules, guessing and using imagination.

Good to know: the best game is the one everyone can join. Adjust rules so that younger, older or less confident players can take part.

Classic group games and how to play

Kho-kho

A traditional tag game from South Asia played by two teams. Nine players of the chasing team sit in a row facing alternate directions while one chaser tries to tag the runners. It develops speed, quick turns and teamwork.

Seven stones (Lagori / Pittu)

One team throws a ball to knock down a stack of flat stones, then tries to rebuild the stack while the other team tries to hit them with the ball. It trains aim, coordination and cooperation.

Musical chairs

Chairs are set in a circle, one fewer than the number of players. When the music stops, everyone sits. To keep it kind and inclusive, let players who sit out become helpers or music controllers.

Treasure hunt

  1. Hide small objects or clue cards around a safe area.
  2. Split players into small teams of different ages.
  3. Give each team a clue that leads to the next one.
  4. The team that follows all clues first wins, though a shared celebration works too.

Antakshari

Two teams take turns singing a song that begins with the last letter of the previous one. It works well at gatherings and requires no equipment.

How to start a community play circle

A play circle can be a weekly meeting of neighbours, parents, school friends, a club or a community group. Keep it simple at the start.

  1. Choose a purpose. For example, outdoor games for children on Sunday mornings.
  2. Find a safe place. A park, school ground, community hall or courtyard, with permission where needed.
  3. Pick a regular time. A fixed schedule makes it easy for people to join.
  4. Invite openly. Share the plan with neighbours, school groups and friends, and welcome all abilities.
  5. Prepare three to four games. Mix active and calm games, with a short break for water.
  6. Assign helpers. Adults should supervise, especially when children are present.
  7. Ask for feedback. Adjust the games and timing based on what people enjoy.
Equipment tip: most group games need only a ball, rope, chalk, cones or everyday objects. Low cost keeps the circle open to everyone.

Safe, fair and inclusive play

Safety

  • Check the play area for stones, glass, holes and traffic before starting.
  • Keep water available and take breaks, especially in hot weather.
  • Match activity to age and ability, and avoid rough contact games with mixed ages.
  • Keep a basic first-aid kit and a contact number for parents or guardians.
  • Children should always be supervised by responsible adults.

Fairness and inclusion

  • Mix teams so that stronger and weaker players are balanced.
  • Never single out a child for losing or making a mistake.
  • Offer different roles, such as scorekeeper or judge, so everyone can contribute.
  • Adapt rules for children with disabilities so that they can take part fully.
  • Praise effort and good behaviour more than winning.

Playcircle does not promote gambling, betting or games played for money.

Frequently asked questions

How many players do I need for a group game?

Many games work with as few as four players. Others, such as relay races or capture the flag, are better with eight or more. You can always adapt the rules.

What can we play indoors when it rains?

Charades, Simon says, musical statues, word chains, card and board games, quizzes and indoor treasure hunts all work well in a small space.

How can I include a shy child?

Start with small teams, give them a simple role they are comfortable with, and avoid putting them on the spot. Confidence usually grows with friendly encouragement.
